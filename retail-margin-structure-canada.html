Retail margin structure in Canadian grocery

Margin versus markup, how a price moves from factory to shelf, and the trade spend that sits on top.

Published 26 October 2026. 4-minute read.

Margin and markup are not the same

The most common pricing mistake in grocery is confusing margin with markup. Retailers and distributors talk in margin: the share of their selling price they keep. Markup is the percentage added to cost.

To reach a selling price from a cost and a target margin, divide: selling price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). Adding the margin percentage to cost understates the price every time.

How a price moves from factory to shelf

An imported product typically passes through four commercial steps: the manufacturer’s export price, the landed cost in Canada, the price a distributor or importer charges the retailer, and the shelf price the retailer sets. Each party applies its margin to its own cost.

Example, using illustrative figures for a 400 g jar of sauce:

StepPer jar (C$)
Landed cost in Canada1.96
Distributor margin at 25%: 1.96 ÷ 0.752.62
Retailer margin at 35%: 2.62 ÷ 0.654.03

A shelf price of about C$4.03 lands just above the C$3.99 price point. If the brand needs C$3.99, someone has to give: the factory price, the freight, the distributor or the retailer. Running this calculation before talking to buyers is what makes a pricing conversation credible.

Margins vary by channel and category

Margins differ by retailer, category, pack type and the work each party does. A distributor that delivers to hundreds of small stores needs more margin than one shipping full pallets to a chain’s warehouse. Frozen and chilled products carry higher handling costs than shelf-stable goods. Build your model with the margins you have been quoted, or ranges you can defend, and test the result at the price points shoppers expect.

What the distributor margin pays for

Brands sometimes see the distributor’s margin as a cost to minimize. It pays for real work: buying and holding inventory, warehousing, picking and delivering small orders, extending credit to retailers and collecting payment, and often a sales team that keeps products listed and on promotion. When comparing distributors, compare the service as well as the margin.

Trade spend sits on top of margin

The margin structure describes everyday pricing. Most grocery programs also carry trade spend, funded by the brand:

  • Listing or slotting fees to add new items
  • Promotional allowances paid off-invoice, by bill-back or per unit scanned
  • Advertising and flyer fees for featured placement
  • Display and merchandising costs
  • Deductions for damaged goods, short shipments or compliance issues

Express trade spend as a share of gross sales and track it by account. A program that looks profitable at list price can lose money once trade spend is included.

Price points matter

Shoppers and retailers think in price points such as C$2.99, C$3.49 or C$3.99. A calculated shelf price a few cents above a price point usually ends up either rounded up, slowing sales, or rounded down, squeezing someone’s margin. Design the price chain to land just below the price point you want.

Promotional maths

A temporary price reduction is usually funded by the brand and passed through the chain. Using the example above, taking the shelf price from C$4.03 to C$3.49 is a reduction of about C$0.54 per jar to the shopper. If the retailer keeps its margin percentage on the promoted price, the brand must fund roughly C$0.35 per jar at the retailer’s cost level. The question is whether the extra units sold during and after the promotion cover that cost.

How exchange rates move through the chain

For imported products priced in US dollars or another foreign currency, a weaker Canadian dollar raises the landed cost, and each margin in the chain then applies to that higher cost. As a rule of thumb from our landed-cost worked example, every cent added to landed cost becomes about two cents at the shelf. Decide in advance who absorbs exchange-rate movements and at what point prices are reviewed.

Where there is room to negotiate

  • Factory price, especially with committed annual volumes
  • Pack size and case count, which change freight and handling per unit
  • Payment terms, which change working capital rather than margin
  • The mix of everyday price and promotional funding
  • Logistics: delivering full pallets to a warehouse rather than mixed cases to stores

Read the payment and freight terms

Terms change the real price as surely as the list price does. An early-payment discount, such as 2% if paid within 10 days, reduces what the seller actually receives. Delivered pricing includes the seller’s freight cost; pick-up pricing shifts it to the buyer. Compare offers and margins on a like-for-like net basis, after discounts, allowances and freight.

A checklist for your model

  • Work backwards from the shelf price shoppers will pay
  • Use margin, not markup, at every step
  • Include freight, duty, brokerage and warehousing in landed cost
  • Add expected trade spend and deductions
  • Test the result against exchange-rate and freight changes
