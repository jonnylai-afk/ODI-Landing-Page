Canadian grocery market entry checklist

Eight steps for food brands planning a launch in Canadian grocery, from category fit and labels to pricing, channels and the first year.

Published 26 October 2026. 4-minute read.

1. Confirm the category fit

Walk the shelves where your product would sit. Note the competing brands, pack formats, price per unit and how much space the category gets in different store formats. Then answer the question every buyer will ask: why should a retailer add this item, and what will it bring that the current range does not?

  • Who buys it, and in which banners and regions?
  • Which price tier does it belong in?
  • Is it new to the category, a better version of something there, or a cheaper alternative?

Success in one country does not guarantee demand in Canada, and the Canadian market is regional: what sells in Greater Vancouver may differ sharply from Quebec.

2. Secure an importer and the right registrations

Most food imported for sale into Canada must be imported by a business holding a Safe Food for Canadians (SFC) licence. The importer also needs a business number with an import account and must be set up with the Canada Border Services Agency. Decide early whether you will use an existing licensed importer or set up your own.

3. Get the product and label ready

  • English and French on all mandatory label information
  • The Canadian nutrition facts table in the current format
  • Ingredient list and allergen declarations, including Canada’s priority allergens
  • Net quantity in metric units
  • Importer or dealer name and address as required for imported food
  • The front-of-package nutrition symbol, where the product meets or exceeds the thresholds for saturated fat, sugars or sodium
  • Any claims checked against Canadian rules

Complex products or claims may need a regulatory specialist. Budget for at least one round of label artwork changes.

4. Build the price waterfall

Work from your factory price to the shelf price, step by step. Every party in the chain needs its margin, and the result still has to be a price shoppers will pay.

Promotional allowances, listing costs and deductions come off the top, so test the numbers with promotions included. Our landed-cost worked example shows the calculation with figures.

5. Choose your channel order

ChannelTypical role in a launch
Asian and international groceryOften the fastest route for Asian brands; shoppers already know the category
Independent and regional grocersSmaller volumes, quicker decisions, a place to build sales history
Multi-unit grocery chainsScale, with category review calendars, listing requirements and promotional commitments
Wholesale and distributionReach into many smaller stores without serving each directly
FoodserviceVolume on specification, often in different pack sizes

A phased launch lets you learn without overstretching production or working capital.

6. Prepare buyer documentation

  • Sell sheet with the reason to list, pricing and promotional plan
  • Specifications, GTIN barcodes, case and pallet details
  • Suggested retail price and margin structure
  • Samples and, where possible, sales evidence from other markets
  • Production lead times and a credible supply plan

Keep every claim substantiated.

Ask each target account for its new-item requirements early. Many retailers have their own item set-up forms, product data standards and supplier onboarding steps that must be completed before the first purchase order can be issued.

7. Plan supply and inventory

Ocean transit, customs clearance and warehouse receiving add weeks to every replenishment. Plan safety stock in Canada so a delayed container does not leave shelves empty in the first months, when retailers judge new items most closely.

Agree reorder triggers with your importer or distributor, and know how long it takes from placing a production order to having goods available in a Canadian warehouse. That total, not the ocean transit time alone, sets how much stock you need to hold.

8. Manage the first year

A listing is the start. Track distribution, sales per store per week, promotional results and deductions from the first month. Forecasting, replenishment and follow-up decide whether a product earns repeat orders and survives its first category review.

Questions buyers will ask

  • Why should we list this item, and which item should it replace or sit beside?
  • What is the suggested shelf price, and what margin does it leave us?
  • What will you spend to support the launch, and when?
  • Where else does it sell, and how fast?
  • Can you supply consistently, and how long is your lead time?
  • Who is the importer and who delivers to our warehouse?

Having written answers to these before the meeting is the simplest way to be taken seriously.

A realistic sequence

  1. Category and price check, and a landed-cost model
  2. Importer and distribution arrangements agreed
  3. Canadian label artwork approved and printed
  4. Sell-in kit, samples and target account list prepared
  5. Buyer presentations timed to category reviews
  6. Item set-up, first purchase orders and the first shipment
  7. Launch support, then monthly tracking and quarterly reviews

Who does what

PartyMain responsibilities in a launch
Brand ownerProduct, label artwork, export price, supply and marketing budget
ImporterSFC licence, customs clearance, compliance with Canadian food rules
Broker or representativeAccount strategy, buyer presentations, listings, promotions and reporting
DistributorInventory, delivery to stores or warehouses, invoicing and collections
RetailerShelf space, pricing to shoppers, promotions and replenishment orders

One company can cover several of these roles. What matters is that every role is covered and that each party knows where its responsibilities end.

Common mistakes

  • Pricing from factory cost forward instead of from the shelf price backward
  • Printing Canadian labels before the importer and retailer requirements are confirmed
  • Agreeing to promotional programs without modelling their cost
  • Launching in too many channels at once

General information, not legal, tax or regulatory advice. Requirements change; confirm the rules for your product with a qualified advisor.
