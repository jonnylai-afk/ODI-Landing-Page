Commercializing a food brand for Canadian grocery

Turning a good product into a grocery program: commercial readiness, the sell-in kit, terms, launch support and the first twelve months.

Published 26 October 2026. 4-minute read.

A product is not yet a program

Retail buyers list programs, not just products. A program is a product with a defensible price, reliable supply, a reason for shoppers to buy it, a plan to support it and a team to manage it. Commercialization is the work of building those pieces before the first buyer meeting.

Check commercial readiness

AreaReady when
ProductFormulation, shelf life and pack format are final and tested
ComplianceCanadian label approved; importer licensed; claims substantiated
Cost and priceLanded cost modelled; shelf price and margins hold at the target price point
SupplyCapacity, lead times and safety stock in Canada planned for launch volumes
IdentityGTIN barcodes issued (in Canada through GS1 Canada or your home GS1 organization) and product data complete
SupportLaunch promotion and marketing budget agreed

Write the positioning in one sentence

Before any sell sheet, agree a single sentence that says who the product is for, what it replaces or improves, and why the shopper will pay its price. Every later decision, from pack size to promotion depth, should be tested against it.

Build the sell-in kit

  • Sell sheet: the reason to list in one page, with pricing, margins and the launch plan
  • Category story: the gap the product fills, using shelf and sales evidence
  • Item data: specifications, case and pallet details, GTINs, shelf life, images
  • Samples: retail-ready, from a production run
  • Support plan: promotions, demos, flyer features and digital support for the first months

Price for the long term

An introductory price set too low is hard to raise later, and a launch that only works at a promoted price is not a program. Set an everyday price the brand can hold for years, then use time-limited promotions to drive trial. Keep a margin buffer for the cost increases, freight changes and exchange-rate moves that will come.

Get the product data right

Retailers set up new items from supplier data: GTINs, dimensions and weights for each unit, case and pallet, ingredient and allergen information, images and shelf life. Errors cause delays, wrong shelf tags and rejected deliveries. Treat the item data as part of the product and check it as carefully as the label.

Choose launch accounts deliberately

The first accounts set the brand’s track record. Choose retailers whose shoppers match the product, where the brand can support the launch properly, and where sales data will be visible. A strong result in a smaller account is a better argument for the next listing than a weak result in a large one.

Set prices and terms

Publish a list price and standard terms, then hold them. Consistent pricing across accounts protects the brand’s position and avoids accounts discovering they pay more than a competitor. Payment terms, minimum orders, delivery terms and promotional funding should be clear before the first purchase order.

Launch with support

New items get a limited window to prove themselves. Plan the first promotion, in-store demonstrations where they make sense, and enough stock to avoid out-of-stocks in the first weeks. An item that is out of stock during its launch rarely recovers its velocity.

Budget the launch

Cost areaExamples
Listing and onboardingListing fees, item set-up, supplier onboarding requirements
Launch promotionIntroductory price reductions, flyer features, displays
Shopper activationIn-store demonstrations, sampling, digital and social support
InventorySafety stock in Canada and the working capital it ties up
ManagementAccount management, reporting and deductions control

Shopper marketing that works in grocery

Most grocery purchase decisions happen in the store. Support that earns its cost usually works at the shelf or close to it: flyer features, secondary displays, in-store sampling, and retailer digital programs that reach the chain’s own shoppers. Broad awareness advertising rarely pays back for a new item in limited distribution. Spend where the product is sold, and measure the result by account.

Manage the first twelve months

PeriodFocus
Weeks 1–8Shelf placement checks, stock levels, early sales by store
Months 3–6Velocity against category averages, first promotional results, deductions review
Months 6–12Annual plan with the account, assortment decisions, preparation for category review

What decides the first category review

Buyers look at velocity against the items around yours, the margin the retailer earns, whether your sales are new to the category or taken from existing items, and how reliably you supplied. Brands that track these measures from the first week arrive at the review with answers; brands that do not usually arrive with excuses.

Common reasons new items are delisted

  • Velocity below the category average after the launch period
  • Out-of-stocks or late deliveries during launch
  • A shelf price the category could not support
  • No promotional support after the introductory period
  • Sales taken from the retailer’s existing items rather than new to the category

Decision gates

Set go and no-go points in advance: the shelf price must hold, a launch account must be secured, velocity must reach an agreed level by a set month. Gates protect the brand from spending into a program that is not working, and they make the case for investment when it is.
