How to source Canadian food products for export

A buyer’s guide to specifying, qualifying, quoting and landing Canadian food and beverage products in your market.

Published 26 October 2026. 4-minute read.

Why buyers source from Canada

Canadian food carries a strong reputation for safety and consistency, backed by a federal food safety system under the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) that can issue export certificates many destination markets require. Canada also has trade agreements with many markets, including the CPTPP and CETA with the European Union, plus several bilateral agreements. For products that meet the agreements’ rules of origin, these can reduce or remove import duties in the destination market.

Two cautions. Preferential duty applies only to goods that qualify as Canadian under the relevant rules of origin, not to every product sold in Canada. And “Canadian” on its own does not make a product admissible in your market; your market’s rules still apply in full.

Start with a specification, not a supplier list

Define the exact product, ingredients, pack size, packaging, certifications, destination country, required shelf life on arrival and order volume. A clear specification is what lets suppliers quote on the same basis, and what lets you compare their answers. It also stops a sourcing project drifting toward whatever a supplier happens to have in stock.

Qualify suppliers before samples

Check capacity for repeat orders, export experience, food safety certification (for example BRCGS, SQF or FSSC 22000, which are GFSI-benchmarked schemes) and any halal, kosher or organic certification your market needs. Ask whether the supplier has shipped to your country before and which documents they can provide. This takes a few days and saves weeks of sampling suppliers who cannot deliver.

Build an RFQ suppliers can answer the same way

  • Price basis (EXW, FCA or FOB) and currency, with the validity period
  • Case pack, case dimensions and pallet configuration
  • Minimum order quantity and production lead time
  • Production capacity for repeat orders
  • Shelf life at production and remaining shelf life at shipment
  • Ingredient, allergen and nutrition statements
  • Label options: export label, sticker or bilingual Canadian label
  • Export documents the supplier will provide
  • Payment terms

Labels for your market

A Canadian retail label is bilingual English and French and follows Canadian rules, which rarely match the destination’s requirements exactly. Options include an over-sticker applied before export, a dedicated export label, or a private-label pack. Custom labels usually carry a minimum run, so confirm it before you plan volumes.

Certificates and destination requirements

Confirm registration, labelling, certificate and importer requirements in your market before you order. Depending on the product and market, shipments may need a CFIA export certificate, a certificate of free sale, a certificate of origin to claim preferential duty, and halal or other certificates. Your importer usually knows the local requirements; your Canadian partner coordinates what must come from Canada.

Quality checks and traceability

Ask for a certificate of analysis where the product calls for one, and agree how lots are coded so any batch can be traced. For first orders, a pre-shipment check of labels, packing, pallet build and documents catches problems while they are still cheap to fix. Keep retained samples from each lot until the product has sold through.

A lower ex-factory price can still produce a higher delivered cost if the pack size, pallet fit, shelf life or freight terms are worse.

Payment and risk

New supplier relationships often start with a deposit and balance before shipment, or a letter of credit for larger orders. As trust builds, terms can move toward documentary collection or open account. Match the terms to the order size and the history between the parties, and agree them before production starts.

Common pitfalls

  • Comparing quotes on different price bases, currencies or pack sizes
  • Discovering the minimum label run only after the order is placed
  • Assuming Canadian origin qualifies for preferential duty without checking the rules of origin
  • Too little shelf life left by the time goods reach the shelf in your market
  • Pallets built to Canadian standards that do not fit your market’s warehouses or containers efficiently

Start with a trial order

A first order sized to test the product in your market, the supplier’s reliability and the paperwork is cheaper than a large first commitment. Agree in advance what the trial must prove, then scale up with confidence.
