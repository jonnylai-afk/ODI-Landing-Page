How to find a food broker in Canada

What a broker does, how brokers are paid, how they differ from distributors and importers, and what to check before you appoint one.

Published 26 October 2026. 5-minute read.

What a Canadian food broker does

A food broker represents a manufacturer or brand to buyers. In grocery, that usually means calling on retail head offices and distributors, presenting new items, negotiating listings, planning promotions and following up on orders and deductions. In foodservice, it means working with distributors and operators to get a product specified on menus and into distributor inventory.

The scope varies widely. Some brokers make introductions and little else. Others act as the brand’s full commercial team in the market, handling account planning, promotional calendars, retail execution and reporting. Before you compare brokers, decide which of these you need, and get the agreed scope in writing.

Broker, distributor or importer

The three roles are often confused, and the difference decides who carries inventory, credit risk and regulatory responsibility.

RoleOwns the goods?Typical responsibilities
BrokerUsually notSales calls, listings, promotions, account follow-up and reporting
DistributorYesBuys, stores, delivers and invoices retail or foodservice customers; carries credit risk
ImporterYesBrings goods into Canada, holds the Safe Food for Canadians licence and answers to the CFIA for compliance

Many companies play more than one role, and an overseas manufacturer usually needs all three covered. A broker alone cannot get product onto a Canadian shelf if no one is licensed to import it or willing to hold stock and invoice retailers. Ask each candidate how the other two roles will be filled.

How grocery listings work in Canada

Multi-store grocery chains buy centrally. New items are usually presented to a category manager at head office, often during a scheduled category review, and approved items are set up in the retailer’s systems with full product data before the first order. Most chains receive product through their own distribution centres; some categories and smaller retailers are served by distributors or direct store delivery. A broker’s value lies in knowing each account’s calendar, paperwork and people, and in preparing a brand to meet them.

Types of brokers

  • National retail brokerages with teams covering the large grocery chains across Canada. Strong account access, but smaller brands can struggle for attention.
  • Regional and independent brokers focused on one province or region, often with close relationships at regional chains and independent grocers.
  • Specialty and ethnic-channel brokers who know Asian, South Asian, Latin or other international grocery chains and their distributors, which buy and promote differently from mainstream retail.
  • Foodservice brokers who work with broadline and specialty foodservice distributors and with restaurant groups.

Match the broker to the channel where your product will actually sell first, not to the channel you hope to reach eventually.

How brokers are paid

Retail brokers are most often paid a commission on net sales in their territory, sometimes with a monthly retainer while the brand is building distribution. Some charge project fees for specific work such as a market assessment or a trade-show program. Whatever the model, agree on what the commission is calculated on (gross or net of deductions and promotional allowances), which accounts it covers, and which costs, such as samples, travel and trade shows, are paid by the brand.

Six things to check before you appoint a broker

  1. Category experience. Have they sold products like yours, to the buyers you want? Ask for examples by category, not client names.
  2. Channel coverage. Which retailers, distributors and regions do they actually call on, and how often?
  3. Retail economics. Can they walk you through distributor and retailer margins, promotional costs and typical deductions for your category?
  4. Import and distribution answers. Who will import, hold stock and invoice customers? A good broker has an answer before you ask.
  5. Sample and materials handling. Who prepares sell sheets, item set-up forms and buyer presentations, and who manages samples?
  6. Follow-through. Who manages the account after the first listing, and how and how often do they report?

Broker or your own sales team?

BrokerOwn sales team in Canada
CostMostly variable, tied to salesFixed salaries, travel and office costs
SpeedExisting account relationships from day oneMonths to hire and build relationships
FocusShared across the broker’s portfolioDedicated to your brand
ControlManaged through the agreement and reportingDirect
Best forMarket entry and brands below the scale that justifies a teamEstablished brands with significant Canadian volume

Many brands start with a broker and add their own people as volume grows. Build that possibility into the agreement’s term and termination clauses.

Questions to ask in the first meeting

  • Which accounts would you present us to first, and why?
  • What shelf price do you think our product needs, and what does that imply for our export price?
  • Who would import and distribute the product?
  • Which category review dates are coming up for our category?
  • What will you report to us each month?
  • Which brands in our category do you already represent, and how do you handle conflicts?

Red flags

  • Guaranteed listings or sales volumes before anyone has seen your price and label
  • Large upfront fees with no milestones attached
  • No clear view of the landed cost and shelf price your product needs
  • Vague answers on who imports, stores and invoices
  • No reporting format, or reluctance to put territory and accounts in writing

Prepare before you reach out

Brokers decide quickly which brands to take on. Have these ready:

  • A product catalogue with specifications, ingredients, origin and certificates
  • Case configuration, pallet pattern, shelf life and GTIN barcodes
  • An export price list (EXW or FOB) and a suggested Canadian shelf price
  • Your current label, and a view of what must change for Canada: English and French text, the Canadian nutrition facts table, allergen declarations and, where it applies, the front-of-package nutrition symbol
  • Your supply capacity and lead times

Label changes are often the longest step in the timeline, so start them early.

Agree the terms in writing

TermWhat to settle
Territory and channelsProvinces or regions; retail, wholesale and foodservice; named accounts if needed
ExclusivityWhether exclusive, for how long, and what performance keeps it
CompensationCommission basis, retainer, project fees and who pays which expenses
House accountsAny customers the brand keeps for itself
ReportingFormat, frequency and the measures that matter
Term and terminationInitial term, review dates, notice period and commission on sales after termination

What the first 90 days should look like

Measure progress through milestones you can verify: label and price readiness, a target account list, buyer presentations booked and held, distributor discussions and samples in buyers’ hands. Listings follow category review calendars, so judge a broker on the quality of this work before judging them on orders.
