Landed cost worked example: one container from Asia to Vancouver

A line-by-line calculation for a container of shelf-stable sauce, from FOB price to shelf price, with the three sensitivities that matter most.

Published 26 October 2026. 4-minute read.

The assumptions

All figures are illustrative assumptions for one product and one shipment, not a quote. Freight rates, exchange rates and duty rates change, and duty depends on the product’s tariff classification and origin.

AssumptionValue
ProductShelf-stable sauce, 12 × 400 g jars per case
Quantity1,800 cases in one 40-foot container
FOB priceUS$14.00 per case
Exchange rateC$1.38 per US$
Ocean freight to VancouverUS$2,600 per container
Cargo insurance0.3% on 110% of cost and freight
Duty rate6% (assumed for illustration)
Customs brokerage and processingC$450
Port, terminal and drayage to a Burnaby warehouseC$1,400

Two details worth noting. Canada generally calculates duty on the value of the goods at the point of direct shipment, which is why the duty line excludes ocean freight. And GST is charged at import on taxable goods but not on most basic groceries; it is also generally recoverable by GST-registered importers, so it is left out of the landed cost here.

Where the money goes

ComponentShare of landed cost
Goods (FOB)82.0%
Ocean freight8.5%
Duty4.9%
Brokerage, port and drayage4.4%
Insurance0.3%

For a dense, relatively high-value product like this, the factory price dominates. For low-value, bulky products such as snacks or paper goods, freight can be a far larger share, and container fill matters more than a few cents on the factory price.

From landed cost to shelf price

StepPer jar (C$)
Landed cost1.96
Distributor at 25% margin: 1.96 ÷ 0.752.62
Retailer at 35% margin: 2.62 ÷ 0.654.03

The result sits just above the C$3.99 price point, so the plan needs a small saving somewhere in the chain, or a shelf price above C$3.99 that the category can support.

Container utilization

Freight, brokerage and port charges are largely fixed per container, so the number of cases you fit in it changes the cost of every jar.

Case dimensions, pallet pattern and whether pallets are used at all can change how many cases fit. Check the load plan before you agree the case configuration with the factory. Our FCL load and freight calculator works out container utilization and freight per case for up to nine products at once.

The three sensitivities that matter most

ChangeLanded cost per jarShelf price per jar
Base case1.964.03
Ocean freight doubles to US$5,2002.13 (+0.17)4.37 (+0.34)
Canadian dollar weakens to C$1.45 per US$2.06 (+0.10)4.23 (+0.20)
Duty falls to 0% under a trade agreement1.87 (−0.10)3.83 (−0.20)

Because each margin is applied to the cost before it, every cent added to landed cost becomes roughly two cents at the shelf. That is why freight and exchange-rate risk belong in the pricing conversation from the start.

Full container or shared container

For small first orders, less-than-container-load (LCL) shipping avoids paying for empty space, but charges per cubic metre, adds handling and usually takes longer. As volumes grow, a full container lowers the cost per unit, as the table above shows. A consolidated container, combining several products from the same region, can bridge the gap.

Working capital

The landed-cost model shows what the goods cost, not when you pay for them. Between a deposit to the factory and payment from your customer, cash is tied up through production, ocean transit, customs clearance, warehouse time and the customer’s payment terms. Add those stages up for your route and terms, and include the cost of that cash in your pricing decisions.

Using the model in negotiations

A transparent landed-cost model changes the conversation with factories and buyers. With the factory, it shows exactly how much a pack-size or price change is worth at the shelf. With buyers, it shows that the proposed shelf price is built on real costs. Keep it updated with each new freight quote and exchange-rate move.

Other costs to include for some products

  • Inspection and holds: CFIA or CBSA inspections can add fees, storage and delay
  • Demurrage and detention: charges when containers stay at the port or with the importer beyond the free time
  • Wood packaging: pallets must meet the ISPM 15 treatment standard, or the shipment can be refused or ordered treated
  • Relabelling in Canada: applying compliant stickers after arrival adds labour and time per unit
  • Cold chain: reefer freight, power at the terminal and frozen storage for chilled and frozen goods

Check these before you rely on the numbers

  • The product’s tariff classification and whether preferential duty applies, with the proof of origin it requires
  • Whether the product falls under a supply-managed category such as dairy, poultry or eggs, where over-quota duties can exceed 200%
  • Current freight quotes, including surcharges, for your route and season
  • Pallet fit and container utilization for your case dimensions
  • CFIA requirements and any inspection costs for your product
